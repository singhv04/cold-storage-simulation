What's modeled physically: a per-zone W/kJ energy balance (envelope UA × ΔT, doorway infiltration, shift heat, defrost heater load, minus compressor cooling capacity ÷ COP) integrated against each chamber's real air + product thermal mass — not arbitrary °C/min constants. On top of that sits a closed twin loop: synthetic noisy/lossy telemetry, an online estimator that recalibrates capacity and UA against it, anomaly detection that tells equipment drift apart from sensor faults, a rolling forecast from the calibrated model, and a window.ColdStorageTwin API for an external AI/optimization layer to read state and issue commands. Design summary — what this actually is, in plain words

No jargon first — read this before trusting any number this tool shows you.
⚠
In one line: this is a pretend cold storage, run entirely by math, that tries to guess its own condition the same way a real monitoring system would. There is no real building, no real compressor, no real sensor anywhere behind this page. Everything — the temperature, the truck arrivals, the workers, the readings on the "sensors" — is calculated. But it's calculated in two separate layers on purpose: one layer plays the role of "the real building" (which we never let the smart layer peek at directly), and a second layer plays the role of "the smart monitoring system," which only ever gets to see noisy, sometimes-missing readings — just like a real facility's software would. That second layer then has to guess, learn, and flag problems using only what it was allowed to see. That's the whole trick that makes this a digital twin instead of just a simulation.

How it's built, step by step

  • Step 1 — a pretend building. Every chamber is modeled as three connected "buckets" of heat: the wall, the air, and the stored product. Heat flows between them the same way it would in real life — warm outside air leaks through the wall, the wall warms the air, the air warms or cools the product depending on whether the compressor is running. This pretend building also "ages" — the compressor slowly loses a bit of its cooling power the more hours it runs, and the sensors slowly drift out of calibration, exactly like real equipment does over months of use. Nothing breaks suddenly; everything wears out gradually.
  • Step 2 — pretend sensors. The smart layer is never shown the pretend building's true temperature. It only gets a "sensor reading" — a slightly wrong, slightly delayed, occasionally missing number, the way a real wall-mounted sensor and a real WiFi/network connection would behave.
  • Step 3 — a second, independent guess. The smart layer keeps its own private guess of what the temperature is and how strong the compressor really is. Every few seconds it compares its guess to the sensor reading, and if they disagree, it nudges its guess closer to reality — a bit like slowly turning a dial until two clocks agree.
  • Step 4 — deciding what's wrong, not just that something's wrong. If the smart layer's guess keeps disagreeing with the sensor no matter how much it adjusts, it tries to figure out why: has the compressor genuinely gotten weaker (equipment wearing out), or is the sensor itself lying (a calibration problem)? It only raises a flag once it's fairly confident which one it is — not on every single noisy reading.
  • Step 5 — looking ahead. Every so often, the smart layer runs its own guessed version of the building forward in time to predict what's about to happen over the next 3 hours — that's the dashed line on the temperature chart.
  • Step 6 — giving advice. A simple rule-checker looks at all of the above and writes plain-English suggestions ("defrost early," "book a service visit," "pre-cool before the price goes up"). This is deliberately the easiest part to replace with a real AI model later — it already has clean, structured data to read from.
  • Step 7 — a door for other programs. Everything the smart layer believes is available through window.ColdStorageTwin in your browser's console — so another program (or a real AI agent) can read the current state, read the forecast, read the advice, and even trigger events, without touching any of the internal code.

What's still a shortcut (said plainly)

  • Each chamber is treated as one well-mixed room — it doesn't know that the back corner might be colder than the middle, the way a real chamber can.
  • The weather, the electricity price, and the compressor's efficiency numbers are all reasonable guesses written by hand, not pulled from a real weather service, a real electricity bill, or a real machine's spec sheet.
  • Spoilage is estimated with one general formula (temperature too high, for too long, spoils food faster) rather than a formula specific to each exact product tested in a lab.
  • The "guessing" logic (Step 3 above) is a simplified version of what real industrial software uses (called a Kalman filter) — it works and settles on the right answer, but it isn't the mathematically optimal version.
  • Random events (which day a truck shows up, how noisy a sensor reading is) aren't reproducible — running the simulation twice won't give identical results.
  • Cooling and dehumidifying aren't properly split: a real compressor spends part of its cooling power just condensing moisture out of the air (this takes real energy — it's not free), which should mean slightly less power left over to actually drop the temperature. Here, humidity is tracked as its own separate number and doesn't borrow any capacity from the temperature calculation — so on a very humid day, this sim is a little more optimistic about cooling power than a real system would be.
  • A frosted coil is penalized twice — once as less cooling actually delivered, and again as worse electricity-to-cooling efficiency. Both effects are real and independently documented in refrigeration engineering, but applying both at once means a badly frosted coil is modeled as somewhat worse than most single real-world measurements of frost's impact — a deliberately conservative (not an accidental) choice.

What's genuinely good about it

  • The smart layer actually has to earn its beliefs from noisy data — it isn't secretly told the answer.
  • There's a real, working "plug" (window.ColdStorageTwin) another program can connect to today.
  • When something looks wrong, it tries to explain why, not just sound an alarm.

What it can't do yet

  • It isn't connected to anything real — so nothing it says applies to an actual building until you connect it to one.
  • Nobody has checked its numbers against a real cold room's real logbook.
  • All 3 chambers still try to hold the SAME temperature (whichever product you pick) — a real facility would run each chamber at its own separate temperature, but that isn't built yet, even though which chamber gets which delivery is now handled correctly.

What it would take to connect this to a real building

  • Swap the hand-guessed building size, wall insulation, and compressor power for your actual building's real drawings and equipment spec sheets.
  • Replace the pretend sensor readings with a real feed from your real sensors (a technical detail: the code calls this feed stepTelemetry() — only that one function needs to change).
  • Replace the hand-written weather and electricity-price guesses with a real weather service and your real electricity bill's rate schedule.
  • Replace the simplified guessing math with proper industrial-grade estimation software, and replace the plain-English advice rules with a trained AI model.
  • If it matters for your product, add the ability to model cold and warm spots inside a single chamber, instead of treating each chamber as one uniform room.
